Kelvin waves and sea level: the pulses that carried the warm water east

Bursts of westerly wind send pulses of warm water east along the equator. Each raises sea level by a few centimetres, so daily satellite sea level shows every wave crossing the Pacific and the warm water piling up near South America; tide gauges then follow the waves up the coast to Washington.

The wind bursts

Short bursts of westerly wind along the equator set off the Kelvin waves (Pulses of warm water that travel east along the equator beneath the surface, crossing the Pacific in about two months.).

The Kelvin waves

Each burst sends a pulse of warm water east, visible as a rise in sea level.

Along the coast from Ecuador to Washington

When a Kelvin wave reaches South America, part of it turns north and south along the coast.

Data (all public, from NOAA):

  • Daily satellite sea level (blended altimetry): NOAA NESDIS, via NOAA CoastWatch ERDDAP
  • Daily surface winds (Blended Sea Winds v2): NOAA NCEI
  • Daily tide-gauge sea level: University of Hawaii Sea Level Center
  • Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI): NOAA Climate Prediction Center
